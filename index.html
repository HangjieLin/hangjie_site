Hangjie Lin

Coastal geoscientist and PhD student in Earth & Environment at Boston University.

I study what satellites can and can’t see in fast-changing coastal water, how sediment moves from the shelf into bays and marshes, and whether those marshes can keep pace with rising seas.

About

I work with Cédric G. Fichot at Boston University, supported by a NASA FINESST award. Before Boston, I studied marine geoscience at Nanjing University with Qian Yu, starting on the Liyashan oyster reefs and moving on to reading coastal turbidity from space.

I still like to start in the mud. Field instruments, satellite records and hydrodynamic models all end up in the same sediment budget.

My research follows a connected set of questions. What can satellites reliably observe in fast-changing coastal waters? What controls the movement and storage of water and sediment? And how do these processes shape the long-term fate of coastal landscapes?

Satellite tidal dynamics and the physical scaling of bays and estuaries

Three manuscripts in preparation.

SWOT1 gives the first two-dimensional view of coastal water levels from space. I use it to map the central 90% water-level range (WLR90)2 along the world’s coasts, to quantify how bays transform the amplitude and phase of the tide, and to test the dimensionless controls set by basin geometry, friction and inertia.

A related line asks whether global tide products underestimate tidal range close to shore, and what that would mean for assessments of wetland resilience.

1 Surface Water and Ocean Topography, launched in December 2022. Its KaRIn radar maps water height across two 50 km swaths on either side of a 20 km gap.

2 The range between the 5th and 95th percentiles of water level: what a site sees most of the time, without the extremes.

Coastal observability, remote-sensing bias and time-series reconstruction

Satellites see the coast in snapshots, and the snapshots are not random. Beginning with unevenly sampled ocean-color records, I developed ways to recover tidal, fortnightly and seasonal sediment variability from sparse observations.

That work grew into diagnosing when satellite views misrepresent dynamic coastal water (cloud–wave selection effects,3 sun-synchronous tidal aliasing,4 uncertainty in inferred sediment fluxes) and into physically informed strategies for extracting reliable process information.

3 Optical sensors can’t see through cloud, and cloudy days tend to be the windy, wavy, turbid ones. Clear-sky archives under-sample them.

4 A sun-synchronous satellite passes at the same local time every day. It sees the solar S2 tide at almost the same phase on every pass, and the K1 tide (23.93 h) aliases to a one-year period that looks just like a season.

Sediment transport, connectivity and budgets from estuaries to wetlands

Two first-author manuscripts under review, at Nature Communications and Nature Geoscience.

Building on tidal sediment variability, I combine satellite records, in situ observations and hydrodynamic models to quantify net tidal exchange, near-bed sediment properties and sediment availability along the land–ocean continuum.

Current regional work follows persistent change in U.S. East Coast bays, and asks how much offshore sediment can still reach Louisiana’s wetlands as relative sea level rises.

Coastal eco-geomorphology and landscape resilience

My earliest field and remote-sensing studies looked at oyster-reef geomorphology, tidal-flat bars and salt-marsh margins. Collaborative work now connects marsh-edge erosion, vegetation boundaries and sediment accumulation.

At the global scale, I ask why some salt marshes persist or expand while others decline. Tidal exchange, sediment availability and wave-driven mobilization act together, and reading them together gives process-based indicators of vulnerability and resilience.
